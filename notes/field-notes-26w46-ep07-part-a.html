AI Is a Tool — Keep the Judgment

This is Part A of Field Notes 26W46-EP07. This half is a quiet pep talk — not a scare story. Tools keep getting better at carrying mental load. That can free you. The risk is quieter: you stop checking. Part B — Map → JSON covers the data side of last week's floor-plan idea.

Offloading is fine

We're not claiming science has proved that AI permanently lowers IQ. That claim is contested and too neat. The steadier word is cognitive offloading — handing memory or drafting to a tool so your head stays free. Offloading is normal. Losing the habit of review is the problem.

Keep estimation

You've heard a version of this with calculators. The useful lesson wasn't "ban the tool." It was keep estimation — a feel for whether the answer could be right.

Ten times ten will not be a thousand. If the display says something wild and nobody flinches, the tool didn't fail first. The check did.

A short case — Mata v. Avianca (2023)

Same pattern showed up in court. In Mata versus Avianca, a filing cited cases that did not exist. The model had invented them. When counsel asked whether those cases were real, the model kept saying yes.

Courts didn't punish "used AI" as a crime. They punished filing without verifying — and without being candid when things fell apart. The lesson travels: draft with help; gate with a person.

Habits that travel

Treat AI as a draft machine. You are still the editor. Before anything leaves — email, report, number, citation — a human signs off.

Estimate first. If a figure or a case name feels odd, stop and check. Ask where you would verify — a primary document, an official database, a real person — not only the chat that wrote the line.

And write the "do not" list before you unload work: no invented numbers, no treating output as fact, no self-shipping. Boundaries make offloading safer.

If I had to put Part A in one line: AI is a tool. Offload the load. Keep the judgment. Check before you ship.
